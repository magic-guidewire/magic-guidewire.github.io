MAGIC · Magnetic Actuation for Guidewire Intelligent Control

Slack-Aware Feeding with Learned Load Anticipation for Magnetic Guidewire Navigation

Simulation study · SOFA discrete elastic rod · patient-derived vessels · 80 mT

Code Paper · in preparation

Abstract

Pushing a magnetically steered guidewire through tortuous vessels often fails not because the tip points the wrong way, but because the wire stops advancing: proximal push piles up as slack, wall loads rise and the passage stalls. We study this in a SOFA discrete-elastic-rod simulation of a magnetic guidewire navigating patient-derived vascular anatomies at 80 mT. Stalls are announced by push–progress slack, and wall loads grow with slack rather than with feed speed. We therefore regulate the proximal feed with two layers on top of an unchanged magnetic steering law: a slack-feedback law that throttles the feed as slack grows, and a learned load anticipator, a network trained on recorded passages that predicts the wall and head loads of the next 3 s for each candidate feed from the mechanical state and the route geometry ahead, and picks the fastest feed whose predicted loads stay within limits. On 24 training-patient routes the controller reached the target on all 24 and lowered the median peak wall load by 47% relative to a constant slow feed while being 13% faster. On 28 routes from 11 unseen patients, learned anticipation lowered the peak wall load by 29% relative to slack feedback alone (one-sided Wilcoxon p = 3×10-6) at a 2.5% longer passage, and by 21% relative to a uniform slowdown of equal duration.

Method

Slack

Slack is the commanded proximal push minus the route progress of the tip (its arc length along the route centerline). It is cheap to measure from the tip position and is the variable that precedes every stall.

Slack feedback

The feed runs at full speed (5 mm/s) while slack stays below 3 mm and is reduced linearly to 0.2× at 10 mm. The magnetic steering law and the feed filter are unchanged.

Learned load anticipation

A multilayer perceptron (3 × 256, SiLU) maps 26 mechanical and route-geometry features and a candidate feed to the wall and head loads of the next 3 s. It was trained on 438k decisions from 1,244 recorded passages, with patient-grouped folds so that every evaluated route uses a model that never saw its patient.

Why passages stall

With a constant feed, every stall is a build-up of slack: on 24 training-patient routes the maximum slack exceeded 25 mm in all 28 stalled constant-feed passages but in only 4 of 68 successful ones. Below, the same route at constant full feed (slack grows until the wire stops) and with slack feedback (slack stays in the set-point band).

Wall load grows about tenfold with slack at either feed speed, while at a given slack between 0 and 40 mm the full feed changes it by at most 1.6×: slowing down helps mainly by keeping slack small.

Unseen patients

The frozen controller on 28 routes from 11 patients that appear in no training data. Slack feedback alone reached 28/28, with learned anticipation 27/28, the constant slow feed 25/28. Learned anticipation lowered the peak wall load on 23 of 27 routes reached by both: paired median 0.71× (95% bootstrap CI 0.53–1.00, one-sided Wilcoxon p = 3×10-6) at 1.025× the passage time.

Against the contact-damped feed baseline on the same 28 unseen-patient routes: with K=3 it reached 26/28; on the 26 routes reached by both, learned anticipation was 0.97× its passage time with 0.45× its peak wall load (lower on 19 of 26, one-sided p = 4×10-4). With the weaker damping K=1 it reached only 22/28; on the 22 routes it did pass it was faster (our passage time 1.14× its time), while our peak wall load was 0.35× its peak (one-sided p = 2×10-4).

Longest routes: the 14 remaining unseen-patient routes longer than 255 mm (long aortic arches, where the constant feed stalls) were run once with the frozen laws. The constant slow feed reached 1/14, slack feedback 13/14 and with learned anticipation 13/14 (exact McNemar p = 5×10-4); learned anticipation lowered the peak wall load on 13 of 13 routes (paired median 0.59×, p = 1×10-4) at 1.016× the passage time. Over all 42 unseen-patient routes: constant feed 26/42, slack feedback 41/42 (p = 6×10-5), with anticipation 40/42, peak wall load 0.63× (p = 6×10-10).

Is it the anticipation, or just going slower?

Learned anticipation slows the feed where the model expects high loads, so it adds a few percent of passage time. To separate anticipation from slowing down, each route was re-run with slack feedback slowed uniformly by a per-route factor that matches the anticipating controller's passage time. At equal time, anticipation lowered the peak wall load by a further 21% (paired median 0.79×, lower on 16 of 17 routes). The load model predicts the 3 s wall-load maximum of unseen patients with R² = 0.97 (head load 0.62, progress 0.81).
